A cube spacecraft with eight thrusters (layout of Sims & Wolek, Table 3) must deliver a net thrust. Many different thruster burns give the same net force, but each leaves a different torque for the reaction wheels to cancel. Theory: why the current allocation can cost the wheels more than it needs to

This page does not give you a better allocation. It shows you how the current one is chosen, what it ignores, and what the alternatives look like. Finding and formulating the better one is the exercise.

1 · Two linear maps from the same eight numbers

Let βi = Ti/Tmax ∈ [0,1] be the throttle of thruster i, which fires along the body-frame unit vector ai from position ri (paper, Table 3). Everything the spacecraft feels from the thrusters is linear in β:

force b = T_max · A β A = [ a₁ … a₈ ] (3×8) paper Eq. 36–37 torque τ = T_max · C β C = [ r₁×a₁ … r₈×a₈ ] (3×8) paper Eq. 10 fuel flow ṁ = (T_max / I_sp g) · Σβᵢ

The trajectory optimizer asks for a net thrust b. Three equations, eight unknowns: for a given b there is a 5-dimensional family of throttle vectors, namely the polytope {β : Aβ = b/Tmax, 0 ≤ β ≤ 1}. Every dot in the pictures above is one point of it. Same force, different torque, different fuel. The wheels never see b; they see τ.

2 · What the current allocation optimizes

Eq. 38 of the paper picks β by

min ½‖T_max Aβ − T‖² + ½λ‖β‖² s.t. 0 ≤ β ≤ 1, λ = 10⁻⁶ T_max²

When the demanded thrust is producible the first term is driven to zero and the regularizer decides which of the infinitely many exact solutions you get: effectively min ‖β‖² subject to Aβ = b (the λ→0 limit; this page computes exactly that). Look at what is, and is not, in the objective:

  • The torque Cβ does not appear. The torque is a by-product. Where it lands in the cloud is decided by the layout and by a regularizer that was added to make the answer unique, not to make it good for the wheels.
  • Fuel is not in it either, not exactly. Fuel is Σβi (an ℓ₁ quantity); the regularizer is Σβi² (ℓ₂). Minimizing ℓ₂ spreads throttle over several thrusters, because splitting a load lowers a sum of squares. When those thrusters have partly opposing components, the spread buys cancellation: ΣTi exceeds ‖T‖ by exactly the thrust that cancels. That is the “canceling thrusters” effect from the abstract, and the plot above shows it as a gap between the dotted line and the current dot.

So the current allocation is a feasible point chosen by a criterion that is neither the fuel nor the wheel torque. Unless that criterion happens to coincide with a good trade-off between the two, there are other feasible points that are better on one axis, the other, or both. The plot of fuel against torque shows whether it does: any dot below and to the left of the current marker is an allocation that is better on both counts.

3 · Why the wheels care

With the attitude held on the pointing profile, the wheels must supply −τ for as long as the thrusters fire. Two limits matter: peak torque on each wheel axis (the |τ1|, |τ2|, |τ3| columns, with one wheel per body axis) and accumulated angular momentum ∫τ dt over the burn. The paper assumes ideal wheels with no torque or momentum limit (its Table 2), so a high τ is invisible to the current formulation. A real wheel set would saturate. A trajectory that is fuel-optimal but needs more wheel torque than exists is not flyable.

4 · What is special about this layout

  • Thrusters 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 8 sit at cube corners with axis-aligned directions. Each has a lever arm of 0.5·√2 ≈ 0.71 m, so each produces torque 0.71·Ti as well as force. Thrusters 4 and 6 fire along a body diagonal through the center of mass: zero torque.
  • Thrusters come in opposed pairs, (1,7), (2,8), (3,5), (4,6): equal and opposite forces. Pairs (1,7), (2,8), (3,5) have the same torque as each other, so firing such a pair gives zero force and a doubled torque.
  • Because of the corner placement, almost any force you ask for drags some torque along with it. The question is how much of that is unavoidable (layout) and how much is the choice of β.

5 · What this page does not do

  • It is a single instant: one body-frame thrust vector, no time history. In the real problem b(t) is re-expressed in the body frame as the attitude changes (paper Eq. 35).
  • Pointing torque and gyroscopic terms (the other pieces of Eq. 9) are ignored; wheels are unbounded; gravity-gradient is zero as in the paper.
  • The dots are the vertices of the feasible polytope (black) plus random convex combinations of 2–4 of them (grey); all give exactly the requested net thrust. They show the shape of the set. They are not an optimizer, and they are not a uniform sample: uniform samples of this polytope are mostly absurd burns in which opposed thrusters fire against each other, so you would see only the far corner of the plot.
  • Numbers use Tmax = 680 N and Isp = 1960 s from the paper’s Table 2. You can change them in the sidebar. Nothing here has been run inside the paper’s trajectory optimizer.

Exercises

  1. Verify the torque column of Table 3 by hand for thrusters 1, 4 and 8, and compare with the sidebar’s torque arrow when you demand exactly +x at 0.5 Tmax.
  2. Demand +x, magnitude 0.5. Which thrusters does the current allocation fire? Which components of their forces cancel? Use the “wasted thrust” column to confirm.
  3. Click dots in the fuel-vs-torque plot that sit left of or below the current dot. In the throttle bars, how do their patterns differ from the current one? In torque space, where are they relative to the origin?
  4. Use the direction map. Are there directions where the current allocation is already among the lowest-torque ones? Where is it worst? Is there any thrust direction that can be produced with essentially zero wheel torque, and why?
  5. Raise the magnitude toward the edge of what is producible. What happens to the cloud, and what does that tell you about the freedom you have in allocation near saturation?
  6. Open problem (not provided here). Write down an allocation problem that keeps b as a constraint and has both fuel and wheel torque in the objective, with a weight you can sweep. Decide which norm to use for each term and why. Solve it in Python (e.g. SciPy or CVXPY), reproduce this page’s dots for a given b, and place your solutions on the fuel-vs-torque plot. Then ask what changes once the burn is a trajectory and not one instant.
