The theory behind what you just watched

No background assumed. Each part below matches something on the screen, and every equation is explained in words first.

0 · The big picture

We want a map of the sea floor (the bathymetry) but we can only measure depth at the places our robots actually swim. So two questions matter: (1) how much of the area did the robots sweep? (coverage) and (2) how good is the map we can build from those few measurements? (mapping error). Changing where the quadrotor drops the robots changes both, which is what you can explore here.

1 · Getting the robots there: the quadrotor

A quadrotor stays in the air by pushing air downward with four propellers. To hover the total thrust must equal the weight, T = m g. Heavier aircraft need more thrust, and the power needed grows faster than the mass: roughly P ∝ m3/2. That is why the model charges more power when the quad is carrying all its robots and less after each release.

  • Energy vs. power. Power (watts) is how fast energy is used. Energy (watt-hours) = power × time. A battery stores a fixed amount of energy (here about 74 Wh), so flying longer or heavier uses a bigger fraction of it. We only plan to use about 80%, to keep a safety reserve.
  • Speed profile. A drone cannot change speed instantly. It speeds up at a limited acceleration (2 m/s²), cruises, then slows down. Plotted as speed against time this makes a trapezoid, so a short hop never reaches full cruise speed.
  • Flight phases. spin-up, climb to release altitude, transit, hover and release, return, descend, land. The panel above the plots tells you which phase it is in.
  • Release. The robots fall slowly under a small drogue (about 5 m/s), so a 30 m drop takes about 6 s, plus a few seconds to settle on the water before they start swimming.

2 · Moving a tiny boat: how the robots swim

Each robot is modelled like a toy car (a unicycle): it has a heading ψ (the direction it points), a forward speed v and a turn rate ω. Its position changes as

ẋ = v cos ψ + cx,   ẏ = v sin ψ + cy,   ψ̇ = ω

(a dot means “rate of change per second”). The terms cx, cy are the water current, which pushes the robot sideways whether it likes it or not.

The robot controller does not set v and ω directly. It asks for a speed and a turn rate, and the tiny DC motors take time to respond because propellers and motors have inertia. A first-order lag captures this:

v̇ = (vcmd − v) / τ

In words: each second, the speed closes a fraction 1/τ of its gap to the requested value (τ is the time constant, here about 2 s for speed and 0.7 s for turning). That is why the paths are smooth and the boats loop through corners instead of snapping. Small random noise in heading and speed adds the wobble you see in real vehicles.

3 · Finding a path around obstacles

Grid and obstacles. The area is chopped into 1 m × 1 m cells, and each cell is either water or blocked (land or an obstacle polygon). Obstacles are inflated by a 3 m safety margin so the planned line keeps clear of them, since the real boat will not follow it perfectly.

A* search. To get from A to B the planner explores cells and keeps, for each, f = g + h: g is the distance already travelled to reach it, h is a cheap optimistic guess of the distance still to go (straight line). It always expands the cell with the smallest f, so it heads toward the goal but can still go around walls. A final pass removes unnecessary zig-zags, leaving the dashed lines you see.

Waypoints. If you add waypoints, the robot treats them as a to-do list: the first one is the goal; when the robot gets within a few metres of it, the next one becomes active, and so on until the collection point.

Following the path (pure pursuit). The robot looks at a point a few metres ahead on the path and steers toward it, like a driver looking down the road instead of at the bumper. The steering command is proportional to the angle error between where it points and where that point is.

4 · The sonar footprint

Each robot carries a downward-looking sonar. Sound spreads out in a cone of full angle β, so the patch of sea floor it “sees” is a circle whose radius grows with depth d:

r = d · tan(β / 2)

Deep water gives a wide footprint, shallow water a narrow one. Two things to try: widen the beam angle and watch coverage climb faster, and note that boats in deeper parts of the map sweep more area per metre travelled.

5 · Metric 1: coverage

Every ~half second each robot “paints” the circle under it on the grid. Coverage is the share of all water cells that have been painted at least once:

coverage (%) = 100 × (cells swept) / (all water cells)

Cells under obstacles and land are not counted. Coverage can only go up. It is the teal shading on the mission map. High coverage means you have looked at a lot of the area. It does not by itself say how well you can reconstruct the map, which is metric 2.

6 · Metric 2: mapping error, via a Gaussian process

The problem. The robots measure depth at a few thousand points, but we want depth everywhere. We need to guess the depth between and beyond the measurements. The key physical fact: sea floors are smooth. Two places 1 m apart almost surely have nearly the same depth. Two places 100 m apart may not.

The kernel. A Gaussian process (GP) turns that fact into a formula. The kernel says how similar the depths at two places are as a function of their distance Δ:

k(Δ) = σf² · exp( −Δ² / (2ℓ²) )

Similarity starts at its maximum for Δ = 0 and falls smoothly. The length-scale ℓ sets how fast: features smaller than about ℓ are treated as unpredictable, larger ones as learnable. σf says how much the depth typically varies (in metres).

The estimate. Given measurements y at locations X, the GP predicts depth at a new place x* as the average depth plus a weighted combination of how far each measurement is from that average. Closer, more informative measurements get more weight:

ŷ(x*) = ȳ + k(x*, X) · ( K + σn² I )−1 · ( y − ȳ )

Here K collects how similar the measurement points are to each other (so two nearly identical measurements are not counted twice), and σn is the sensor noise (the GP will not chase every jitter). Far from any data, the correction fades away and the map falls back to the plain average depth, so unexplored areas look flat. The picture below shows a one-dimensional version: orange dots are measurements, the blue line the estimate, the shaded band the GP’s own uncertainty, which pinches in near data and balloons where there is none.

Mapping error (ME). Because we invented the sea floor ourselves, we know the truth, so we can score the map. ME is the typical size of the mistake, the root-mean-square error:

ME = √( (1/N) Σ ( ŷi − di )² )

Take the estimated depth ŷ and true depth d at each of N test points in the water, square the differences (so over- and under-estimates both count), average, and take the square root to get metres back. The “|Error|” button shows these mistakes as a map: bright means a bad guess.

Why ME can wobble. New data usually helps, but the GP’s “average depth” is the average of what has been seen so far. If the first robots sample mostly deep water, the fallback average is too large everywhere else, and ME can briefly rise before it recovers. That is normal, not a bug.

7 · What to try

  1. Same robots, different layout. Apply “Line”, then “Cluster”, run each, and use Pin run to overlay the curves. Which gives lower ME for the same number of robots?
  2. Forcing a sweep. Add waypoints that make one robot cross the unexplored basin before heading home. Coverage and ME both improve, at the cost of a longer swim.
  3. Sensor width. Raise the beam angle from 90° to 120°. Coverage rises, but does ME improve as much?
  4. GP length-scale. Set ℓ to 5 m (too small: the map is patchy) and to 60 m (too big: details are smoothed away). The true bathymetry has features around 15–35 m, and the best ME is near that.
  5. Sample rate. Lower it to 0.1 Hz: measurements are 3 m apart instead of 0.3 m. Does the final map get much worse?
  6. Energy. Add robots and watch the quadrotor’s Wh. More robots take more flight time and the quad is heavier on the way out.

8 · Honest limitations

  • The sea floor is synthetic (smooth bumps plus a tilt), so a GP with the right length-scale does well. Real seabeds are rougher, and ℓ would have to be learned from data.
  • Robots do not avoid each other; the current is a simple model; propeller and hull forces are lumped into time constants.
  • To keep the browser fast, a new measurement within ℓ/20 of one the GP already holds is skipped. This hardly changes the map, but it means very high sample rates stop helping.
  • The quad’s power numbers are plausible for a ~1.5 kg quadrotor but not from a specific vehicle. They are editable.
